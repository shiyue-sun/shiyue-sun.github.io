Why build this

Marketers built the audience in Treasure AI. Then they left to send the message.

Marketers used Treasure AI (then called Treasure Data) to unify customer data and build segments. To actually reach those people, they exported the segments to a separate email tool. That hand-off added work, and it gave customers a reason to move to platforms that did both. Engage Studio closes the loop, so a campaign goes from segment to send in one place. Most customers would be migrating from mature tools like Braze, so products with a decade’s head start set the bar for the experience.

Design deep dive

Two decisions, in detail.

Where complex editing should happen, and how marketers find the email they’re looking for.

01

Modal editor vs. full-page editor

Every complex editor in Treasure AI’s customer data platform opened in a modal. When I started designing Engage Studio, I challenged that pattern for two reasons.

Users weren’t sure what they could touch.

We heard feedback that users were confused about whether they could interact with the content underneath the modal.

Complex tasks need room.

A full-page editor gives marketers more space to focus on the task in front of them.

The decision

I explored both a modal and a full-page design, then collected feedback from the UX team. We decided that every complex editor in Engage Studio would use a full page.

A new pattern for the product

Full-page editing was new to the product, so I built it alongside the Diamond Design System work.

02

Layouts I explored

Why a table first

It reused an existing pattern, so frontend could build it quickly. For M1, shipping on time mattered more than the ideal layout, as long as the next step was planned.

Why cards next

Marketers recognize an email by how it looks. A preview needs more space than a table row can give, and cards give it that space.

Information architecture

Evolving the navigation as Engage Studio grew.

Over 15 months, Engage Studio added admin permissions, a second campaign type and a new channel. Each time, I reorganized the navigation around how marketers think about their work, not how the system is built.

I used terms marketers already know, and when I couldn’t reach customers directly, I brought in GTM and Product Marketing.

Oct 2024 · Starting point

One left nav for everything

The M1 navigation followed the existing Customer Data Platform pattern: one left nav holding every feature, including Campaigns and Email templates. It worked for one user role and one campaign type.

Jun 2025 · Workspaces and policy-based permissions

Separate one-time setup from daily work

Problem

Admin-only setup, like Workspace and Domain setup, sat next to marketers’ daily work, like Campaigns and Email templates.

Decision

I moved admin configuration into a header above the left nav and kept the left nav for daily work.

Why

It separates what admins set up once from what marketers use every day. I also explored an icon next to the workspace selector as the entry point, but it was too easy to miss, and users might not understand what it meant.

Oct 2025 · Always-on campaigns

Name campaigns by how long they run

Problem

The PRD named the new type “Live campaign” and the existing type “Batch campaign.” “Live” implied other campaigns weren’t live, and “Batch” described how the system sends, not what the marketer wants.

Decision

I checked with the GTM team, then proposed “Always-on campaign” and “One-off campaign.” The PM adopted both.

Why

Both names describe how long a campaign runs, in plain words marketers already use.

Jan 2026 · In-app messages

Make room for a channel that isn’t a campaign

Problem

In-app messages are triggered by a user event and appear in the customer’s mobile app, so they fit neither campaign type. More features like this were on the roadmap.

Decision

I added a new nav section. I proposed “In-product campaigns”; Product Marketing suggested “In-product experience,” and I went with it.

Why

As the product grows, the section needs to hold more than campaigns, such as content cards and transactional experiences.

Why campaigns aren’t organized by channel

Engineering and the PM in Japan decided to keep one-off and always-on campaigns separate, because combining them would significantly increase development cost, and we had promised customers the feature on a tight deadline. I designed the navigation within that constraint.

Working without customer access

I couldn’t talk to customers directly, so GTM and Product Marketing, who work with customers every day, stood in for them. The limitation is that they share our team’s assumptions.

Result

The structure absorbed in-app messages without a redesign and has room for content cards and transactional experiences on the roadmap. Since launch, we haven’t heard complaints from customers about the navigation or the new names.

Process

Fitting design into an agile team.

When engineering’s process changed, I found where design was breaking and fixed it.

Problem

Several scrum teams, one designer, and priorities set in meetings in Japan. Three mid-to-large projects landed on design at once.

Change

Aug 2025. I raised the misalignment with my manager and leadership, and the PM began sharing timelines with me directly.

Sep 2025. I added a “Ready for Dev” status in Figma and a UX changelog in the PRD. Frontend engineers confirmed it helped.

Result

Jun 2026. At the department offsite, I aligned PM, UX research and design. Design and research now attend roadmap meetings, and design starts 2–3 weeks before engineering.
